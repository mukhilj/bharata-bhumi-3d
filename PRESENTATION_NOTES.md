# Presentation Notes — using Bhārata Bhūmi with 6–12 children

These notes connect the app's layers to the geography album. The app is an *aid to
imagination*, not a replacement for charts, stories, or the children's own model-making.
Keep the adult's role the same as with the teacher-made overlay maps: show little, ask
much, and let the research impulse take over.

## The design follows the album

The layer panel deliberately mirrors the **transparency overlay maps** (highlands/lowlands
→ rivers → settlements): begin with the bare land, then lift one page at a time. And it
follows **whole to parts**: always open on the whole subcontinent before flying anywhere.

## Suggested presentation arcs

### 1. The land before names (first presentation)
All layers off, exaggeration ~12×. Simply turn the subcontinent slowly.
"Here is the land we live on, exactly as it was measured from space — every hill and every
hollow of the sea floor." Let them notice the wall of white in the north, the triangle
pointing into the ocean, the wrinkle of the Western Ghats. Name nothing yet; let them ask.

*Key moment:* drag exaggeration to **1×**. The mountains vanish. "This is the truth — on
the whole Earth, even the Himalaya is thinner than the skin on an apple. We stretch the
land upward only so our eyes can study it." (Honesty about the material's isolation —
the exaggeration itself becomes a lesson in scale.)

### 2. The three ranges (follows Formation of Mountains)
Turn on **Three ranges of the Himalaya**, fly the *Himalaya & Karakoram* journey. Trace
Himadri (white line, avg ~6,000 m), Himachal (3,700–4,500 m), Shiwalik (900–1,200 m) —
three waves of the same collision, youngest at the front. Turn on **Peaks** and let
children click the land beside each pin: why does the ground read 8,650 m when the label
says 8,849 m? (Each cell averages a square kilometre — a genuine data-literacy question
for older children.)

### 3. The work of water, written on the land (follows The River)
Turn on **Rivers & lakes**, then **River basins**. Every coloured region is "all the land
whose rain reaches one river." The basin shapes were *computed from the shape of the land
itself* — the same law the children met in The River: water obeys the slope. Ask: where
does a drop falling on the two sides of one ridge go? Find the divide between Godavari
green and Krishna orange. The legend gives areas in lakh km² beside the official figures —
older children can verify the map against the Central Water Commission's numbers, a real
research exercise.

### 4. The old land and the young land
Turn on **Plateaus, plains, coasts & islands**. Contrast the Deccan (ancient Gondwana
rock, worn low and stable) with the Himalaya (young, still rising ~5 mm a year). Show the
**Deccan Traps** outline — lava that flooded out ~66 million years ago, layer upon layer;
zoom into the Western Ghats edge and the children can *see* the staircase ("trap" is
Swedish for stairs). Connects directly to the Composition of the Earth and the story of
the Earth's cooling.

### 5. The sea is not empty
Turn on **Seas & oceans**, tilt low over the Bay of Bengal. Click the sea: 3,000 m deep.
Find the drowned ridge running south (the Ninety East Ridge), the shallow shelf that
joins India to Sri Lanka at Palk Strait, the coral specks of Lakshadweep. The ocean
floor has mountains and plains too — geography does not stop at the shore.

## Follow-up work the app should provoke (not replace)

- Paper/clay models of the three ranges, placed on an outline map (album activity).
- Blue-yarn river tracing on the floor map, then checking courses against the app.
- Research cards: Why is Kangchenjunga India's highest but Everest the world's?
  Which basin do *we* live in? What is a lagoon (find Chilika)?
- Command-card style: "Find a place where a river cuts *through* a mountain range"
  (the Brahmaputra at Namcha Barwa; the Indus gorge) — antecedent drainage, for the 9–12s.

## Cautions

- One layer at a time — the isolation of concept is easily lost on a screen.
- Keep sessions short; the app is a spark. The child's hand-made map is the real work.
- The probe readout gives elevations children can *record* — encourage notebooks, not
  screen time.
